Abstract

This project applies synthetic reading, a method of literary analysis developed by Ghosh, Shanmugapriya T and Mandal (2026) in which interpretation comes before computation, to Ruskin Bond's short story "The Eyes Have It". In the story, a young man who is blind shares a train compartment with a girl and takes care not to reveal his blindness. Only after she has left does he learn, from another passenger, that she was blind as well.

The initial impression was formed after reading only 21 of the story's sentences. Read in isolated blocks, its language of seeing looked like ordinary description, ordinary metaphor and ordinary small talk. The project therefore asks what the metaphor of sight and blindness does across the whole story, and how markers of evasion, assumption and turn-taking reveal the information asymmetry and mutual deception between the narrator and the girl. Four dimensions, with twelve sub-types, were turned into a prompt. An open-weight language model, run locally, extracted phrases from the story in three chunks; — phrases survived word-for-word verification and were counted, — times in all, across twelve scenes, four stages and four voices.

Four claims were stated before the full reading and tested against the counts by explicit rules.

Read through phenomenology, disability studies, pragmatics and conversation analysis, the story suggests that sight and blindness work as a metaphor for knowing other people. The travellers' politeness, guesses and silences let each of them avoid knowing the other, and the final question, "Didn't you notice?", is asked of every reader who assumed that the girl could see.

Research questions

  1. What is the metaphor of sight and blindness across the full story?
  2. How do linguistic markers of evasion, assumption and conversational turn-taking reveal the underlying information asymmetry and mutual deception between the narrator and the girl?

Objectives

  • To apply the seven steps of synthetic reading to a short story in Indian English, keeping every interpretive decision human and documented.
  • To map the language of sight, blindness, performance and concealment across the scenes, the stages and the voices of the story.
  • To trace markers of evasion, assumption and turn-taking to the speaker who uses them, so that the question of mutual deception can be tested in the language itself.
  • To confine the language model to extraction, verify every phrase it returns, and publish what was removed and why.
  • To interpret the patterns through phenomenology, disability studies, pragmatics and conversation analysis.

Claims stated before the full reading

C1
Imagined sight (memory, guesswork, the mind's eye) is concentrated in the middle of the story, where the narrator performs being sighted.
C2
The language of pretence and concealment runs through the conversation with the girl, while the language of revelation is concentrated at the end.
C3
Markers of evasion and assumption appear in the girl's speech as well as in the narrator's narration and speech, so the deception is mutual in the language and not only in the plot.
C4
Turn-taking is densest while the narrator and the girl are talking, and becomes sparser after she leaves.

Each claim has a written test, set before the counts were seen. The verdicts are in Claims.

The story and the corpus

A blind young man is travelling alone towards Dehra in a train compartment. At Rohana, a girl gets in. Unable to see her, he learns about her through sound, and he does not want her to know that he cannot see. They talk about the hills of Mussoorie, the countryside outside the window and her face. She leaves at Saharanpur, a new passenger enters, and the last lines change the meaning of everything before them.

The corpus was prepared from a web transcription of the story. Everything on the page except the story itself was removed. The text was not lower-cased, stemmed or stripped of stop words, because every extracted phrase is checked against the wording of the text.

Method

Synthetic reading moves from a situated human impression to computation and back to interpretation. The seven steps below were carried out in a single Google Colab notebook.

  1. Partial reading

    The sentences were divided into three equal parts, and one-fifth of each part was read: the first seven sentences, seven chosen at random from the middle (seed 42) and the last seven.

  2. Initial impression

    Before the full story was read, the impression, a positionality statement, two research questions, four claims and four dimensions of analysis were written down.

  3. Text upload

    The cleaned story, without its title and byline, was split into sentences and divided into the same three parts, which became the three chunks sent to the model.

  4. Query generation

    The prompt defines four dimensions and twelve sub-types, asks for the shortest verbatim phrase that carries each meaning, and fixes the structure of the JSON answer. Interpretation is excluded.

  5. Targeted extraction

    An open-weight model run locally with Ollama (temperature 0, seed 42, structured output) answered for each chunk. Every phrase was then checked word for word against the story; duplicates and phrases not in the text were removed and listed.

  6. Computation and visualisation

    The phrases were counted in every sentence, longest phrase first, without counting any stretch of text twice, and grouped by scene, stage and voice. Chi-square tests and Spearman correlations describe the spread, and six figures were drawn.

  7. Interpretation

    The patterns were read through literary, linguistic and disability theory, checked against a close reading of the full story, and the four claims were tested by their written rules.

What the fragments suggested

Simulated visuals

“The hills are covered with wild dahlias, the sun is delicious”

In an isolated block, the narrator relies on memory to perform sightedness. Out of context, this reads as a vivid, eyewitness description of a landscape rather than a rehearsed script from the past.

Poetic misdirection

“in my mind’s eye I could see telegraph posts flashing by”

In a random block, this reads as a conventional literary metaphor for daydreaming or deep thought, rather than as a literal admission of a blindness that relies on auditory cues (the rumble of the wheels).

Mundane pragmatics

“Where are you going?” · “You have an interesting face”

Random blocks of dialogue read simply as polite, slightly flirtatious compartment chatter between two strangers, without the underlying tension of mutual concealment.

One overall concept Performed sightedness. In fragments, the language of seeing looks like ordinary description, ordinary metaphor and ordinary small talk. Only the whole story can show whether it is performance and concealment, and by whom.

Positionality

I read "The Eyes Have It" as a sighted reader, and this is the most important fact about my position. The story's ending is built for readers who, like the narrator's second fellow passenger, take sight for granted, and I am likely to share the assumptions that it exposes. I do not speak for blind readers: when I write about blindness, I rely on disability scholarship, including the work of blind writers such as Georgina Kleege, rather than on my own experience.

I am an MA student in Digital Humanities and Social Sciences at IIT (ISM) Dhanbad, trained first in Comparative Literature at Jadavpur University. That training makes me attentive to the gap between what a text says and what it means, and to how meaning shifts between languages and contexts; it also means that I read English literary prose as one of several languages in which I read. Ruskin Bond's Dehra and Mussoorie belong to an Indian English literary landscape familiar to many Indian readers from school, and I tend to approach his stories as gentle and nostalgic, which may lead me to underread their tensions.

My work in digital archiving and AI for the humanities makes me both interested in and cautious about what a machine can find in a literary text. I treat the language model as a reader that notices words, not as a reader that understands them: it extracts, and I interpret.
